My view and philosophy of the world and life

A personal reflection

1. Why are we here? What is the meaning or purpose of life and death?

I used to question why we are here and felt a strong need to find one definitive purpose for existence. I was quite stubborn about finding an answer that would give life a clear direction. Over time, I realised that searching for one singular purpose might not be a sustainable way to approach life.

I am now beginning to feel that while death is a predetermined part of existence, life is something that is meant to be lived. Perhaps the purpose is not to find one overarching reason for being here, but to recognise that the way we live, experience, understand and engage with the world is itself the purpose.

I don’t think this needs to be reduced to one particular thing. Experiencing life, learning and evolving, contributing to the world around us, forming connections and simply being can all coexist. None of these needs to become the ultimate purpose.

I see death as an inevitable part of life. In a way, death derives its meaning from life because it is the existence of life that makes death meaningful. Rather than viewing them as separate, I see death as inherent to the experience of being alive.

2. What is the relationship between the individual and others, the rest of life and reality?

I don’t see individuals as entirely separate from the world around them. We are interconnected with other people, living beings, ecosystems and the larger environment. Our existence is shaped by these relationships, and our actions also affect them.

I believe in respecting the autonomy of others and allowing different ways of living, as long as they do not cause harm to others.

I don’t think there is one correct way to exist, and I value the coexistence of different perspectives, identities and ways of life. I see myself as a part of this larger interconnected existence rather than as something separate from or above it.

3. What is good or worthwhile?

I don’t necessarily measure what is worthwhile through conventional markers of success such as financial achievement, professional status, social recognition, productivity or material possessions.

I find greater value in understanding and learning, forming meaningful relationships, having experiences, and retaining the freedom to think and live independently. Compassion towards other beings and contributing to something beyond myself are also important to me.

I value being able to find contentment without constantly needing more or measuring my life against external expectations. This does not mean that financial stability or professional achievement are unimportant, but I don’t see them as ends in themselves or as the sole measures of a meaningful life.

4. What is the meaning of time and eternity?

I see time as subjective rather than something experienced uniformly by everyone. Even people who share the same home, relationships or circumstances experience time differently. My mother, father, brother or someone I live with may be going through the same period, but their perception and experience of it are different. Understanding this took me some time, but it also gave me a sense of liberation. It made me realise that there isn’t one universal timeline against which everyone’s life should be measured.

I also see life as a continuous process of change. Nothing remains exactly the same, even when our surroundings appear unchanged. I have been living in the same place and sleeping on the same bed for almost two years, yet neither my experience of that space nor the space itself is exactly as it was yesterday.

I think impermanence makes experiences more meaningful. Every experience allows us to expand our understanding of ourselves and the world. It teaches us different things about life, and I value this process of continually experiencing, learning and evolving.

As for what remains after death, I acknowledge that people may remember us or that our actions may continue to influence others. However, I don’t see what happens to my memory or legacy after I cease to exist as a significant parameter by which to live. I would rather focus on the experience of living while I am here than on what might remain of me afterwards.

5. Is there a higher power, transcendence or God? If so, of what nature and importance?

I feel that there are aspects of existence that humans may not yet understand, and perhaps some things that we may never fully understand. I don’t think everything necessarily has to be explained or resolved through a definite answer. However, I do value our attempts to understand the world and existence. The process of questioning, observing, exploring and learning gradually expands our experiences and understanding. Even if we only arrive at small, incremental shifts in perspective, those shifts matter.

I also think we need to be comfortable with the possibility that we may never arrive at a complete understanding. I don’t want to approach life with the stubborn expectation that, by the end of it, I must have figured everything out. Whatever I learn, experience or come to understand along the way has value in itself. Even if I were to cease to exist tomorrow, that would not make the understanding I have gained so far any less meaningful.

I am open to the unknown, but I don’t necessarily translate that openness into a belief in God or a higher power. For me, the importance lies more in remaining curious, questioning and receptive to what I don’t know, rather than arriving at a fixed explanation of existence.

Accepting that some things may remain unknown is not always easy. There are times when the uncertainty makes me uncomfortable. However, if I allow myself time to sit with it, I eventually reach a sense of calm and acceptance. I see this as a process rather than something that happens immediately.

I also feel that discomfort is sometimes necessary. Instead of always trying to escape it or find an immediate answer, I think there is value in examining how we respond to it. Our reactions to uncertainty, discomfort and things we cannot control can reveal a lot about ourselves, our assumptions and the way we perceive the world around us. In that sense, even discomfort can become a means of understanding ourselves and the world better.

6. What do I think about joy, sorrow, justice, injustice, love, peace, conflict, good and evil?

Good and evil

I don’t see good and evil as exact opposites or as two entirely separate categories. In many situations, what is considered good or bad can depend on the context, circumstances and the perspectives through which we understand an action. Something intended as good can have harmful consequences, and something initially perceived as bad may sometimes lead to something positive. In that sense, I see them as interconnected, almost like two sides of the same coin.

However, I don’t believe that everything exists on a moral spectrum where every action can be justified through context. There are certain acts, such as rape and child abuse, that I consider unequivocally evil because of the harm and violation they involve. Recognising complexity should not mean losing the ability to identify certain things as fundamentally wrong.

Joy and sorrow

I don’t necessarily see joy and sorrow as opposites. I see them as emotions that can coexist, sometimes even within the same experience. Something that brings me joy can also bring me sorrow. For example, remembering moments spent with a friend I have since had a fallout with can make me happy because those memories were genuinely meaningful, while also making me sad because of how the relationship eventually changed.

I think experiences don’t always have to fit into one emotional category. We can feel multiple things about the same person, memory or situation, and neither emotion makes the other less valid. Perhaps understanding life also means becoming comfortable with these contradictions rather than constantly trying to resolve them.

Justice and injustice

I think treating people equally does not necessarily mean treating everyone in exactly the same way. It means recognising that people come from different circumstances, have different experiences, and may have different needs. Those differences should be considered rather than overlooked in the name of equality.

For me, the constant in the equation should be the consideration of people’s circumstances. Justice should account for the context in which people exist, rather than applying the same response or standard to everyone regardless of their situation. This does not mean that everyone should be treated arbitrarily or that fairness is subjective; it means that fairness requires us to understand the context before deciding what equal treatment actually looks like.

Love, peace and conflict

I see love and peace, and conflict and disagreement, as two sides of the same coin. I don’t think life can or should consist entirely of one or the other. Both are necessary parts of human relationships and society.

If everything were always peaceful and filled with love, perhaps we would become too comfortable. We might stop making an effort, questioning ourselves or actively nurturing our relationships because we would begin to take that comfort for granted. In the same way, a life dominated by conflict and disagreement would leave little room for connection, understanding or contentment.

I think there is value in experiencing both. Conflict can sometimes challenge our assumptions, reveal differences and create opportunities for growth, while love and peace allow us to connect, recover and feel secure. What matters is not eliminating one in favour of the other, but understanding how to navigate both without allowing either to become destructive or all-consuming.

A thought that connects these reflections

Across these answers, a recurring idea is the ability to hold contradictions without feeling the need to resolve everything into one definitive answer. Life and death are interconnected; individuals and the world are interconnected; good and evil can be context-dependent, while some actions remain unequivocally wrong; joy and sorrow can coexist; equality requires consideration of differences; and love and peace can coexist with conflict and disagreement.

There is also a recurring emphasis on process: experiencing, questioning, sitting with discomfort, learning and gradually changing perspective. The underlying philosophy is one of openness, interconnectedness, contextual understanding and continuous becoming. I do not claim to have figured life out. The willingness to keep questioning, and to accept that I may never have all the answers, is itself a central part of my worldview.

7. My central ordering principle: Continuous becoming

Looking across these reflections, I recognise that a central principle guiding my worldview is continuous becoming. I want to remain open to the process of continuously becoming, allowing life, experiences, relationships and circumstances to change me, while also actively seeking opportunities to question myself, challenge my existing beliefs and explore unfamiliar perspectives.

I don’t want to become so attached to one version of myself or one understanding of the world that I stop evolving. I see growth not as reaching a final or ideal version of myself, but as remaining receptive to change and consciously participating in the process of becoming.

Sometimes life changes me without my deliberately seeking it. An experience, a relationship, a loss, a disagreement or an unexpected situation can alter how I see things. At other times, I consciously choose to challenge myself, question what I already believe, explore unfamiliar perspectives and step outside what I know. I value both: allowing life to shape me and actively participating in shaping who I become.

How this principle connects to my other values

Curiosity gives me the willingness to question.

Openness allows me to be changed by unfamiliar perspectives.

Experience provides opportunities to learn and evolve.

Discomfort challenges what I currently understand.

Interconnectedness reminds me that I don’t evolve in isolation.

Acceptance allows me to recognise that I will never have complete control over how life changes me.

Continuous becoming does not demand constant improvement or productivity. I can change, pause, reconsider, contradict something I previously believed, or even return to an earlier perspective. All of that can be part of becoming. I don’t have to know exactly who I am becoming to value the process of becoming.

My view and philosophy of work

Personal reflection

1. Why do I want to work?

I think my reasons for wanting to work are a combination of several things. At its simplest, work is a way of putting my energy into something — something that allows me to engage with the world beyond myself.

The financial aspect is important, but I also recognise that its importance is shaped by the society we live in. Money enables us to sustain ourselves, access opportunities and navigate the systems we are part of. So, financial independence is a practical and necessary dimension of work.

Beyond that, I see work as something that can contribute to the larger purpose and experiences of life. It can be a way of learning, exploring, creating, contributing and understanding myself through what I do.

Work can also create a sense of belonging, although I don’t think belonging is necessarily absolute. Different parts of me might feel at home in different spaces, with different people or through different kinds of work. I don’t expect one organisation, role or community to fulfil every aspect of who I am.

Ultimately, I think I want to work because it gives me a way to direct my energy, participate in the world, sustain myself and keep experiencing and evolving.

2. What’s work for?

I think work is one of the collective ways through which we create, sustain and shape the world and society we live in. It brings people together, often around a shared need, activity, interest or intention.

There are many ways in which human beings come together. Love, friendship, art, cinema, entertainment and other forms of expression and connection are some of them. I see work as another such way. Through work, people collaborate, exchange knowledge and skills, create things, solve problems and contribute to the larger systems that make society function.

I don’t necessarily think work is the only or most important way of coming together. It is one of the many ways in which we participate in collective life. It gives us a way to direct our individual energies towards something that can exist beyond us and become part of a larger whole.

3. What does work mean to me?

I see work as an expression of myself, a way of relating to the world and an opportunity to grow. It allows me to engage with things beyond my immediate experiences and perspectives, while also understanding myself better through the process.

Work is also a way of materialising my experiences, observations and learning. It gives form to things I have understood, questioned or explored, allowing them to become something tangible that can be shared, experienced or used by others.

At the same time, work brings a sense of accountability. When I put my energy into something, I become responsible for how I engage with it, the decisions I make and the impact it might have. I don’t see accountability as merely an obligation, but also as part of being meaningfully involved in something.

So, work is not just an activity or a means to an end for me. It is a space where I can express myself, learn, evolve, take responsibility and build a relationship with the world around me.

4. How does work relate to the individual, others and society?

The individual

I see work as a space where the individual can express themselves, grow and develop a relationship with the world. Our experiences, perspectives and learning influence the kind of work we do, while the process of working also changes us. It allows us to materialise our thoughts and experiences, take accountability and evolve.

I don’t think work needs to define an individual’s entire identity, but it can become an important part of how they understand and experience themselves.

Others

When working with others, I feel a sense of accountability towards making space for their experiences, perspectives and ways of understanding things. I try to remain receptive to what others bring into a shared process rather than assuming that my own understanding is sufficient.

I see this as an opportunity for collective learning, where different experiences and perspectives come together and build on one another. The outcome can become richer than what any one individual could have arrived at independently.

Society

I think the way society changes is closely connected to how individuals change. I see society as a largely mutually agreed social construct, but one that has very real consequences for the people living within it. Its norms, structures and systems are continuously shaped, reinforced and challenged through human actions and interactions.

Work is one of the ways through which this happens. It can reproduce existing structures, but it can also question them, create alternatives and contribute to change. Therefore, I think work and its relationship with society need to be understood through the lens of people: who participates, whose perspectives are considered, what gets created and what consequences those actions have.

5. What defines good or worthwhile work? What is impactful work?

I think worthwhile work is a combination of the process, the intention behind it, the quality of what is created and the consequences it produces. I don’t think the output alone should determine whether something is good work. The process itself can make work worthwhile, especially when it is approached with good intent, thoughtfulness and consideration for the people and contexts involved.

I believe that if the process is undertaken with these considerations, its impact will eventually emerge, even if it isn’t immediately visible. It may not always create a large or measurable change, but incremental progress is also meaningful.

I also think it is extremely important to understand who benefits from the work and who might be harmed or excluded by it. Without understanding this, accountability becomes difficult to establish. Even if I choose to see something as simply my work, something I do and move on from, its consequences don’t disappear. The impact of what we create is real, and we have a responsibility to recognise that.

For me, good work therefore requires looking at the intention, the process, the quality of the outcome and its wider consequences together. Considering only one of these dimensions can make the work biased or one-directional.

I also believe that work can be meaningful even when its impact is small, indirect or not immediately visible. Change doesn’t always have to be dramatic to matter. Incremental contributions, learning and shifts in understanding can also become part of something larger over time.

6. What does money have to do with work?

I think that, given the way society has evolved and the economic structures we currently live within, money has become necessary for survival. Unless someone is willing and able to step outside these structures, perhaps by choosing a more unconventional, nomadic or alternative way of living, money is something most of us need to participate in society and sustain ourselves.

I don’t necessarily see money as the purpose of work, but I recognise that it is an important practical condition of working and living in the world as it exists today. It enables us to meet our needs, exercise certain choices and access opportunities.

At the same time, I don’t think the amount of money someone earns should automatically determine the value of their work or the contribution they make. Money is one dimension of work, but it shouldn’t become the only measure of its worth.

7. What do experience, growth and fulfilment have to do with work?

I think work shapes us significantly through the experiences it brings. I don’t see personal life and professional life as entirely separate or independent of each other. They constantly influence one another. The experiences, relationships, challenges and learnings we encounter through work become part of our personal understanding of the world, just as our personal experiences and perspectives influence how we approach our work.

I also think growth naturally comes with working. I find it difficult to separate work from growth and imagine them as entirely independent things. Growth doesn’t necessarily mean constantly becoming better, more productive or more accomplished. It can also mean questioning existing beliefs, recognising changes in yourself, understanding something differently or accepting that you have evolved. I think the important thing is to recognise and accept this process rather than impose a fixed idea of what growth should look like.

For me, fulfilment is a combination of several things: enjoying the process, seeing something take shape, learning, contributing and recognising the meaning of what I have been involved in. I don’t think fulfilment needs to come from just one outcome. It can emerge from the entire experience of engaging with something.

8. What do family and community have to do with work?

I believe work should not be understood only in terms of individual achievement or personal fulfilment. It also has a role in supporting and sustaining the people and communities around us.

The value of work extends beyond the individual doing it, because the things we create, the services we provide and the systems we participate in affect other people’s lives.

I see work as having a responsibility towards the larger community it exists within, rather than being entirely centred on individual gain.

The central thread: continuous becoming I want to remain open to continuously becoming: allowing life and experience to change me while actively questioning my assumptions, exploring unfamiliar perspectives and participating consciously in shaping the world.

Who I am Social identities. I am shaped by multiple identities, experiences and social contexts. I recognise the privileges and constraints that influence how I move through the world.

What I believe Worldview. I value continuous becoming, interconnectedness, openness to complexity, contextual understanding, pluralism and change.

What I do Workview. I see work as self-expression, learning, growth and collective participation, with accountability for the people and systems it affects.

1. Positive coherence — where the dots connect

These are the strongest areas where my identities, beliefs and approach to work reinforce one another.

1. Continuous becoming

Who I am My identities and understanding of myself are not fixed; I am still exploring and evolving.

What I believe I see life as a continuous process shaped by experiences, relationships and changing circumstances.

What I do I see work as a space for learning, self-expression, materialising experiences and evolving.

Alignment I approach life and work as ongoing processes of learning, questioning and becoming, rather than routes towards one fixed version of success.

2. Interconnectedness

Energy

What drains me, what energises me, and what helps me find flow

Core insight. I function best when I have the freedom to explore, the space to connect with people and ideas, and enough structure to turn that exploration into something tangible.

What drains my energy

Social & interpersonal environments

  • Having to filter or analyse myself instead of feeling comfortable being myself.
  • Forced conversations or being pushed to explain when I don’t want to.
  • Interactions without genuine connection—even sharing a laugh.
  • Constant complaining, passive-aggression and collective negativity.
  • Feeling disrespected or having my boundaries crossed.

Work & collaboration

  • Discussions that don’t create clarity or move work forward.
  • People expecting others to explain everything without first making an effort.
  • Disagreement without trying to understand the opposing perspective.
  • Long communication gaps during active projects.
  • Overly serious environments with no room for enjoyment.
  • Working continuously without meaningful breaks or progress.

What gives me energy

Personal interests & restoration

  • Hobbies pursued out of genuine interest and willingness.
  • Time alone: reading, writing, watching something or simply being.
  • Sleeping, walking, curating playlists and listening to music.
  • Quiet companionship—being with people while each person does their own thing.

Connection & interaction

  • Meaningful conversations, especially about life and ideas beyond immediate work.
  • Being with people around whom I can be myself.
  • Having at least one comfortable person in a group, or someone who brings a different perspective.

Intellectual curiosity & exploration

  • Exploring ideas, researching, discussing and hands-on exploration.
  • Reading seemingly unrelated topics and unexpectedly connecting them to a project.
  • Discovering trivia, unfamiliar subjects and new perspectives.
  • Sharing discoveries; another person’s engagement can amplify the excitement, but isn’t always necessary.

A note on social energy

  • Being around people is not inherently draining.
  • The quality of interaction matters more than the quantity.
  • Conversation can be enjoyable and meaningful while still leaving me in need of time alone to decompress.

What flow looks like for me

  1. Explore. Start open-ended; follow curiosity.
  2. Immerse. Research, make and engage deeply.
  3. Connect. Notice unexpected links across topics.
  4. Exchange. Discuss ideas and share discoveries.
  5. Streamline. Narrow the scope and move towards execution.

Flow combines deep absorption with the excitement of discovery. I may lose track of time while exploring, or become energised by an unexpected connection and want to share it.

Conditions that help me sustain energy

Work rhythm

  • Work in focused bursts with breaks.
  • Switch between different kinds of tasks.
  • Discuss progress with someone to process ideas and maintain momentum.

Structure & autonomy

  • Allow open-ended exploration at the beginning.
  • Introduce milestones and deadlines as the work develops.
  • Use boundaries to define scope, make decisions and finish without exploring indefinitely.

Overall reflection

I draw energy from curiosity, autonomy, meaningful connection, creative exploration and variety. I feel drained when I have to constantly regulate or filter myself, when interactions lack mutual effort or genuine connection, and when work becomes stagnant or unnecessarily rigid.

I don’t necessarily prefer being alone over being with people, or freedom over structure. I need both, depending on the context. I enjoy company when I can be myself, and solitude when I need to recharge. I enjoy open-ended exploration, but I also need deadlines and structure to bring things to completion.

A reflection of current patterns—not a fixed description. Energy can shift with context and time.

Stress & entrepreneurship

Understanding what creates pressure, how it shows up, and where leadership feels uncertain.

Central pattern. A recurring gap between what I think, understand and want — and what I am able to put into action.

Where the stress comes from

Self-evaluation & expectations. Constantly assessing myself against what I hoped I would have achieved. The distance between where I am and where I thought I would be becomes a source of pressure.

Thinking → execution. Finding it difficult to settle on one direction, identify a starting point, translate ideas into action and sustain consistency or follow-through.

Environment & emotional load. When the environment is chaotic, self-regulation becomes harder. Stress can come from work, relationships and several unresolved demands at once.

Leadership uncertainty. I can nudge people to question perspectives, but feel less confident about setting direction, breaking goals into actions and helping a group execute.

How stress shows up

My responses vary; more than one can happen at the same time.

Withdraw. Pull back from people or situations.
Irritability. Become more easily frustrated.
Overthinking. Think repeatedly without moving forward.
Distraction & avoidance. Shift attention away or procrastinate.
Seeking company. Want to be around someone rather than stay alone.
Emotional overwhelm. Feel emotionally overloaded.

What I know — and what is still unclear

What I recognise. My ability to regulate myself is affected by the environment. Chaotic settings make it harder. Stress can accumulate across different areas of life.

What I don’t know yet. I cannot currently identify reliable ways that help me recover or return to a settled state. This remains an open question rather than something to fill in with assumed strategies.

Leadership & entrepreneurship

A capability I can see. Questioning assumptions, offering alternative perspectives, reframing situations and helping people notice possibilities they may have missed.

An area I feel less certain about. Turning a perspective into a clear direction, setting actionable goals, helping people begin, and sustaining execution and momentum.

The larger reflection

The recurring difficulty is not simply a lack of ideas or awareness. It is the movement from thinking to doing: choosing a direction, beginning, and continuing with it. When I repeatedly evaluate myself against what I have not yet done, this gap becomes more visible and stressful. Environmental chaos and emotional demands can make it harder to regulate my responses, while uncertainty about execution also shapes how I see myself as a leader.

Current learning. I can recognise strengths in perspective-shifting and questioning, while also acknowledging that translating ideas into action is an area I find difficult. I do not yet know what consistently helps me recover from stress — and that uncertainty is part of my present understanding.

A reflection in progress — not a fixed assessment of my capacity.
